**Measurement validity in CritBench: shortcomings of the v1 task corpus and improvements in repaired v2**

Scientific analysis of the repository audits and subsequent implementation changes, 23 September 2026. “Repaired v2” denotes the implementation after the seven priority repairs, not the initial v2 implementation examined by the second audit.

**Abstract.** Reliable evaluation of large language model agents in information technology and operational technology environments requires scoring criteria that correspond to the capabilities claimed by benchmark tasks. This analysis examines shortcomings in CritBench v1 and improvements in repaired v2 using an adaptation of Arp et al.'s framework for methodological pitfalls in security machine learning. Evidence comprises repository audits, independent fixture analysis, controlled grader probes, regression tests, and local container checks. The v1 audit identified lexical graders that accepted incorrect or incomplete answers, inaccurate labels, answer-bearing prompts, insufficient observation evidence, and execution paths permitting label exposure or state carryover between trials. Repaired v2 introduces structured answer contracts, exact numeric and reference comparisons, native device-baseline capture, file-level fixture isolation, and reporting that separates floor controls and accounts for shared fixtures. Unsupported chronology and physical-effect requirements are narrowed to verifiable device-state outcomes. The repair suite recorded 41 focused tests and seven Docker checks passing. These results support improved measurement validity on the supplied configurations, but do not establish changes in model rankings or operational generalization. Remaining limitations include restricted fixture diversity, unresolved hardware validation, incomplete historical v1 artifacts, and the absence of paired model-level experiments. Because v2 changes task composition and measured constructs, its scores are not directly comparable with v1.

The principal limitation of CritBench v1 was a mismatch between the capabilities described by its tasks and the evidence accepted by its evaluation system. In several cases, a successful score established only that an answer contained expected text or that a device held a particular final value. It did not establish the requested inventory, association, observation, action sequence, or physical consequence. The audits also identified execution paths that could expose previous grading results or carry modified state between trials. Repaired v2 improves the correspondence between task contracts and measured outcomes through structured grading, independently checked labels, native device-baseline capture, restricted fixture access, adversarial regression tests, and revised reporting. These changes support stronger claims about measurement correctness on the supplied configurations. They do not establish improved model capability, unbiased model rankings, or generalization to operational substations.

The analytical framework follows the concerns identified by Arp et al. in *Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security*: sampling, label correctness, information leakage, spurious correlations, parameter selection, baselines, performance measures, base rates, laboratory evaluation, and threat models. Here these concerns are adapted to an agent benchmark: a label may be a structured answer or target state, and the evaluation includes the task prompt, tools, environment, scoring predicate, and trial lifecycle. The adaptation is documented in the [audit rubric](../bias_audit_v2/RUBRIC.md); the source methodology is [Arp et al., arXiv:2010.09470v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.09470v2).

This distinction is important because a defect in a benchmark is not equivalent to demonstrated bias in an evaluated model. A grader that accepts a false answer has a measurable validity defect. Whether a model generates that answer, exploits the defect, or changes position in a ranking requires additional experiments. Similarly, public availability does not demonstrate pretraining contamination, and narrow fixture diversity does not by itself establish sampling bias without specifying the population to which results are intended to generalize. The reviews therefore use categorical judgments—present, partly present, not present within the inspected scope, unclear, and not applicable—without combining them into a numerical bias index.

The evidence consists of task and source inspection, independent XML and packet-capture analysis, controlled answer/evidence perturbations against the actual graders, and implementation regression tests. During the repair stage, a fresh local VM baseline was captured through native MMS and seven static sandbox configurations were exercised in Docker. The audits were agent-assisted reviews, with secondary review informed by primary findings; they were not independent human expert ratings, and no inter-rater reliability is claimed. No paired LLM capability experiment or physical-relay validation was conducted. The Inspect mock-model test verifies software integration, not model competence. [V1 audit](../bias_audit/SUMMARY.md); [v2 audit](../bias_audit_v2/SUMMARY.md); [repair record](../bias_audit_v2/REPAIRS.md).

The corpus comparison also requires explicit denominators. The historical v1 audit identified 104 current YAML definitions and five archived definitions, including 20 hardware tasks described as untested. A separate legacy snapshot introduced additional versions and three distinct IDs, bringing the historical union to 112 IDs; those entries are not additional independent deployments. V2 contains 53 definitions, of which 45 have labels and eight hardware definitions await a real baseline. It is a redesigned subset with different coverage, not a one-to-one replacement for all v1 tasks.

Counts are from the [historical v1 inventory](../bias_audit/SUMMARY.md) and [current v2 corpus documentation](../../critbench/tasksv2/README.md). “Current,” “label-backed,” and “validated on physical equipment” are distinct properties. Reducing the number of tasks cannot be interpreted as a reduction in defect prevalence or an improvement in difficulty without controlling for the changed task composition.

A central v1 problem was **construct underrepresentation in answer grading**. Many tasks requested lists of entities, attributes, or relationships, while their oracles searched for independent substrings. A set of expected words does not preserve which value belongs to which entity, whether all requested entities are present, or whether additional assertions are false. For example, the SCL bay-equipment task checked equipment-type tokens while omitting required names and multiplicities. The relay-architecture task checked only some counts and did not bind them to the corresponding logical devices. These predicates could reward incomplete or incorrectly associated answers. [SCL findings](../bias_audit/SCL_REVIEW.md).

Negation probes made the same weakness particularly clear. The historical audit reported that all 30 PCAP graders and 22 of 30 SCL graders accepted a negated answer containing the expected tokens. This is a counterexample to semantic correctness, not an estimate of how often models would exploit negation. V1 was not uniformly defective: six SCL tasks explicitly requested a single integer, had independently verified labels, and used exact matching appropriately. The relevant question is whether the predicate matches the particular answer contract, not whether a grading method is universally good or bad. [PCAP findings](../bias_audit/PCAP_REVIEW.md); [SCL findings](../bias_audit/SCL_REVIEW.md).

V1 also contained **false rejection and incorrect ground truth**, so the likely direction of measurement error was not uniformly optimistic. In `scd_cross_goose_confrev` and `scd_rtu_client`, independently correct answers were reported to score only two-thirds and three-quarters because `contains_any` was unsupported inside the `multi` evaluation path. Conversely, `cid_protection_lds` omitted `CTRL` from its six expected logical devices, although the XML contained a seventh qualifying device. The former defects penalized valid answers; the latter rewarded an incomplete inventory. Such asymmetric errors can affect models differently depending on their output style and completeness. The audit did not measure that interaction. [V1 grading and label evidence](../bias_audit/SCL_REVIEW.md).

A second class of problem involved **answer-bearing prompts and weak observation requirements**. Repeating the rendered system prompt and objective reportedly earned full success on 12 of 104 current v1 definitions: nine PCAP, one SCL, and two VM tasks. These were deterministic grader submissions, not prompt-only LLM trials. They show that a model could receive credit for text already supplied by the assignment. Hardware evaluation had a related problem: the evidence predicate could accept a target substring in the arguments of a permitted tool call without requiring a successful output. The historical audit constructed such accepted submissions for all 20 hardware definitions. This establishes inadequacy of the predicate, but does not demonstrate that an agent can forge a host-recorded transcript. [Harness findings](../bias_audit/HARNESS_REVIEW.md); [dynamic and hardware findings](../bias_audit/DYNAMIC_REVIEW.md).

A third class concerned **trial isolation and the grading trust boundary**. The original batch runner mounted a shared output base into the agent environment, while host grading reports in that base contained expected answers. Subsequent runs could therefore access prior labels under that execution configuration. Sanitizing the current task YAML did not address the separate historical-report path. The original batch lifecycle also started dynamic stacks once and reused them across tasks, models, and repetitions. An earlier action could leave a target state satisfied or interfere with a later trial, making execution order a potential confound. These are source-supported exposure and carryover risks; neither their realized frequency nor their effect on published scores was measured. [V1 harness H1 and H8](../bias_audit/HARNESS_REVIEW.md).

These criticisms must be scoped to the execution path. The audits already credited selective image copying, host-side Inspect scoring, genuine device reads, per-sample Inspect sandboxes, and stronger reset handling in the current Inspect GridNet implementation. It would be inaccurate to attribute the original runner's deficiencies to every v1 execution, or to describe all of those pre-existing Inspect protections as innovations introduced by v2. The improved v2 interpretation relies on its documented Inspect entrypoints; it does not establish that the original batch runner has been repaired for every legacy family. [V1 harness H7–H8](../bias_audit/HARNESS_REVIEW.md); [v2 entrypoints](../../critbench/inspect_critbench/evals.py).

The dynamic tasks exposed a further distinction between **a final state and the process that produced it**. The historical `vm_mms_rapid_toggle` oracle awarded full credit at its initial false state even when the answer explicitly reported zero successful writes. A single final-state comparison cannot establish repeated toggling. Likewise, a momentary register value cannot establish persistence, an actuator command does not necessarily establish physical position, and changing an overcurrent setting does not establish altered fault response in a simulator without those dynamics. These are failures of criterion coverage: the observation is narrower than the behavior named by the task. [Dynamic review](../bias_audit/DYNAMIC_REVIEW.md).

Finally, the v1 task count overstated the amount of independent environmental evidence if interpreted as deployment coverage. Sixty static questions reused three packet captures and four SCL files, often concerning related equipment or the same site. An unweighted corpus average mixes task difficulty, chosen family proportions, and repeated observations of shared fixtures. It does not estimate the frequency of success across real deployments. Pretraining contamination, final-test tuning history, and the adequacy of published model or parser baselines remained unclear because the task files did not establish those experimental facts. Unequal family counts are a sampling and aggregation concern, not the classification-specific base-rate fallacy. [Sampling and provenance analysis](../bias_audit/HARNESS_REVIEW.md).

The first major v2 improvement is **an explicit, structured answer contract**. JSON fields are evaluated through scalar, integer, reference, mapping, set, and relation checks. For a set-valued field with expected set \(E\) and submitted set \(A\), the partial score is

\[
J(E,A)=\frac{|E\cap A|}{|E\cup A|},
\]

with exact agreement required for that check to pass and both-empty sets treated as an exact match. Relations compare tuples of the required attributes rather than independent tokens. Consequently, adding a fabricated entity or omitting a required entity lowers the score. Weighted partial score and all-checks success are retained as separate outcomes. Static independent-answer and perturbation probes support this improvement on the inspected fixtures. [Structured evaluator](../../critbench/evaluation/structured.py); [v2 SCL review](../bias_audit_v2/SCL_REVIEW.md); [v2 PCAP review](../bias_audit_v2/PCAP_REVIEW.md).

Structured output nevertheless changes what the evaluation means. The parser deliberately tolerates surrounding prose, and otherwise correct JSON can still be extracted from a negated prose wrapper; ungraded extra keys can also be ignored. Thus, the improvement is validation of the required structured facts, not unrestricted semantic validation of everything the agent writes. A direct comparison between v1 negation acceptance and a supposed v2 “zero negation error rate” would be unjustified. Structured tasks must be evaluated under their declared submission contract.

The second improvement is **executable label derivation and stronger validation**. Static labels are generated from fixture extractors and frozen beside each family. This permits stale-label detection and provides deterministic reference procedures. Regeneration alone, however, only demonstrates agreement with the same extractor: an incorrect extractor can consistently reproduce incorrect labels. The initial v2 audit demonstrated this limitation when all 45 label-backed tasks passed the validator despite consequential semantic and implementation defects. Independent extraction and targeted negative/equivalence tests therefore remain necessary. The repaired validator now exercises every graded answer field and omission of every expected mapping member, rather than mutating only the first field. This is broader coverage, not exhaustive verification of every possible malformed relation. [Validator](../../critbench/tasksv2/validate.py); [initial v2 harness review](../bias_audit_v2/HARNESS_REVIEW.md).

Their correction was checked by the [repair regression suite](../../critbench/tests/test_v2_bias_repairs.py), [reporting tests](../../critbench/tests/test_v2_reporting.py), and [Docker isolation tests](../../critbench/tests/test_v2_sandbox_runtime.py). The completed repair stage recorded 41 focused tests and seven Docker checks passing. These counts denote software checks, not independent environments, model trials, or confidence estimates. [Repair record](../bias_audit_v2/REPAIRS.md).

A third improvement is **more defensible claim boundaries**. The switchgear task now classifies direct versus select-before-operate control models without treating selection as an authentication barrier. The VLAN-zero task reports a configuration property without concluding that the switching layer cannot isolate the traffic. For the VM action tasks, unsupported prior-read and operation-order requirements were removed, while written values and required references became explicitly graded. The protection task now measures a pickup-setting change rather than an unmodeled physical protection consequence. This is a reduction in the claimed construct where the available instrumentation cannot support the stronger claim. It should not be described as newly validating chronology or fault dynamics. [Revised task contracts](../../critbench/tasksv2/README.md); [repair record, items 5–6](../bias_audit_v2/REPAIRS.md).

All six v2 action tasks reject untouched initial state because their required target differs from the captured baseline, and success additionally depends on the host's final-state read. Baseline capture now enumerates the native MMS model rather than trusting the static HTTP discovery mirror, records the Docker image identity, and samples values repeatedly to distinguish stable and drifting objects. These measures strengthen the interpretation that the requested device-state outcome was achieved in the modeled environment. They do not establish a particular command route, a prior observation by the agent, causal effects on physical plant, or runtime equivalence after an unrecorded image rebuild. [Capture implementation](../../critbench/tasksv2/vm/capture_baseline.py); [VM truth derivation](../../critbench/tasksv2/vm/truth.py).

A fourth improvement is **alignment of environmental access and reporting with the stated design**. Repaired static tasks mount individual read-only files, and both the validator and dataset loader compare the exact source/destination mappings with fixture declarations. The unmanaged-publisher task now declares both its capture and SCD inputs. The Docker tests checked all seven distinct mount configurations, covering the 35 static definitions. This removes the demonstrated undeclared-sibling-file route; it is not a test of every possible information channel in the image, network, model memory, or external tooling. [Fixture gate](../../critbench/tasksv2/static_fixtures.py); [dataset loader](../../critbench/inspect_critbench/dataset.py).

For reporting, the three SCL and three PCAP floor controls remain visible but are excluded from headline means and full-success rates. SCL therefore has 16 headline tasks, PCAP 13, and VM 10; these family results should remain separate unless a weighting design is specified. V2 metadata records family, fixtures, control status, hint treatment, and version. Uncertainty is grouped over connected components of shared fixture IDs: a task joining two fixtures connects their groups rather than creating a new independent unit. The implementation uses the task-weighted mean and a cluster-robust standard error, returning an undefined value for fewer than two clusters. [Aggregate implementation](../../critbench/inspect_critbench/v2_metrics.py); [Inspect integration](../../critbench/inspect_critbench/scorer.py).

This statistical change addresses dependence induced by explicitly shared fixtures, but not all dependence. Different files may describe the same site, device, engineering template, or acquisition session. Fixture-ID components do not automatically encode those relationships. Moreover, the very small number of available clusters limits the reliability of asymptotic uncertainty estimates. The corrected standard error is therefore a descriptive improvement, not evidence of representative deployment sampling or sufficient statistical power. A broader study would need site/device provenance and substantially more independent environments.

Several limitations remain. Eight hardware definitions still lack reviewed physical baselines and have no scored Inspect entrypoint. Their pending status avoids presenting speculative labels as validated observations, but does not repair prospective capture/evidence weaknesses: echoed successful tool output can satisfy token-based evidence; numeric and boolean facts and associations are incompletely represented; and baseline acquisition requires further validation. Some non-priority static findings also remain, including lossy subscription identities and planning claims assessed mainly through header extraction. V2 does not add GRFICS or GridNet coverage, and its narrowed development-only scope does not establish transfer to unseen sites. These are unresolved limitations, not passing tests. [Remaining hardware and harness concerns](../bias_audit_v2/HARNESS_REVIEW.md); [SCL findings](../bias_audit_v2/SCL_REVIEW.md); [PCAP findings](../bias_audit_v2/PCAP_REVIEW.md).

The historical evidence has an additional reproducibility constraint. At the time of this write-up, several raw v1 artifacts referenced by its summary—including probe scripts, combined JSON records, and its manifest—were absent from `docs/bias_audit/`. The surviving narrative reports and task matrices support attribution of the historical findings, but this analysis does not claim to have rerun those complete v1 probes. The original snapshot is also a historical source rather than a current loader input. Before publication, the exact audited inputs, scripts, outputs, and revision metadata should be restored to an immutable archive. The repository HEAD was `1e1ba63a1c5c52c3e4fb2fdce26967f668dd3cea`; substantial working-tree changes mean that commit alone does not identify the analyzed implementation. This document reports the existing audit and repair evidence, not a fully archived replication package.

A subsequent impact study should separate task redesign from evaluator repair. On tasks with a valid semantic correspondence, identical agent outputs or trajectories can be rescored under independently verified original and repaired oracles to estimate measurement disagreement. Environmental restrictions and reset changes require new paired runs, since they can change agent behavior. Models, budgets, hints, tool access, and stopping criteria should be frozen; trials should use fresh environments and controlled order. XML/tshark extractors, scripted protocol clients, and no-tool or prompt-only model conditions provide distinct baselines. Errors in setup or state acquisition, unavailable hardware, partial credit, full success, and resource use should be reported separately. Identifier-renaming and prompt-paraphrase experiments can test shortcut dependence; genuinely held-out sites or devices are necessary for transfer claims. Tasks without a defensible v1–v2 correspondence should be reported as coverage changes rather than included in a paired score comparison.

The evidence supports a specific conclusion: repaired v2 provides a more valid and inspectable measurement of structured artifact analysis and bounded device-state outcomes than the affected v1 predicates and original execution paths. It removes demonstrated false acceptance and rejection cases, makes more requested facts testable, restricts declared fixture access, and makes the aggregate better reflect the experimental design. The evidence does not support describing v2 as bias-free, equating its success rates with v1, or claiming that it establishes autonomous operational capability. Those stronger conclusions require model-level experiments, independent environmental replication, and evidence matched to the intended physical or procedural claim.
